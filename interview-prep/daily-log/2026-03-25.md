# Daily Interview Prep — March 25, 2026

Five questions worked through this session.

---

### Q1. Why do you want to work here / on this team?

**Answer.** Reference something specific you researched: their architecture posts, an open-source project, a product decision you respect, or a stated engineering value. Generic answers ('great company, good culture') signal you sent 50 applications. Match your stated goals to what the team actually needs.

---

### Q2. How do you handle being stuck on a coding problem for 30 minutes?

**Answer.** Say what you've tried and what specifically is blocking, then ask for a nudge (interviewers expect this — silent spinning loses the round). If unsupervised: re-derive from a smaller case, brute-force first then optimize, and always communicate the partial solution and its complexity.

---

### Q3. What does code review actually check for, from a reviewer's view?

**Answer.** Correctness (does it do the spec?), clarity (naming, structure, dead code), tests (are the important paths covered?), and risk (failure modes, rollback, migration safety). As a reviewer, you're also judging the diff's size — a 3000-line PR is itself a process smell worth flagging.

---

### Q4. Tell me about a time you improved a slow system.

**Answer.** Lead with measurement: what the metric was (p99 latency, throughput), how you found the bottleneck (profiling, slow-query log, flamegraph), the fix, and the before/after numbers. 'I added a cache' without the diagnosis story is the answer that loses senior rounds.

---

### Q5. How do you prioritize when everything is urgent?

**Answer.** Impact x urgency vs effort, but first verify the urgency is real (a 'production is down' that's actually a 2% error rate is different). Make the tradeoff explicit to stakeholders rather than silently absorbing it, and leave room for the actual fire when it lands. Then describe how you communicate the deferral.

---

### Q6. What does union-find (disjoint set) do?

**Answer.** It maintains groups of elements with union() and find(). Path compression + union by rank make each operation ~O(alpha(n)), effectively constant. Classic use: detecting connected components and cycles in undirected graphs.

---

### Q7. BFS vs DFS — when do you pick each?

**Answer.** BFS (queue) finds shortest unweighted paths and is level-order; DFS (stack/recursion) explores deep paths, suits topological sort, cycle detection in directed graphs, and tree traversal. DFS can also blow the stack on deep graphs — use an explicit stack if depth is unbounded.

---

### Q8. What is a skip list and why do databases use it?

**Answer.** A sorted linked list with extra 'express lanes' at higher levels, giving O(log n) expected search with much simpler, lock-friendly code than a balanced tree. LevelDB/RocksDB use it for their memtable because range scans are easy and concurrent insertion is cheap.

---

### Q9. What is a doubly linked list and what is its tradeoff?

**Answer.** Each node holds next and prev pointers, enabling O(1) removal if you have the node, and forward/backward iteration. Cost: 16 extra bytes per node and more pointer writes on mutation.

---

### Q10. How do balanced BSTs (AVL / red-black) stay balanced?

**Answer.** AVL enforces height difference <= 1 via rotations after inserts/deletes — tighter, better for search. Red-black allows larger imbalance but uses at most 2 rotations per insert, so it's favored when insert/delete dominate (e.g., Java TreeMap, Linux CFS).

---

### Q11. What is a sliding window and what does it solve?

**Answer.** Maintain [left, right) over an array with O(1) amortized adds/removes from the ends. Solves fixed-size window max/sum, and variable-size problems like 'longest substring without repeating chars' by shrinking when the constraint breaks.
