# Daily Interview Prep — May 07, 2026

Focus: behavioral. 3 questions worked through this session.

---

### Q1. What is transactional outbox and why use it?

**Answer.** To keep 'update DB + publish event' atomic: write the event into an outbox table in the same local transaction as the data change, then a relay (polling or CDC like Debezium) publishes it to the broker. Consumers deduplicate (at-least-once delivery) — this is the standard way to avoid 2PC across services.

---

### Q2. What is the STAR method and why do interviewers prefer it?

**Answer.** Situation, Task, Action, Result — a structured story where the interviewer can see context, your specific contribution (Action is where 'I', not 'we', belongs), and a measurable outcome. It forces concrete evidence over generic claims, which is what behavioral screens actually score.

---

### Q3. How do you answer 'Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate'?

**Answer.** Pick a real technical disagreement, show you steelmanned the other view, made the case with data/prototypes, and ended with a decision (yours or theirs) that you executed well. The signal is how you handle being wrong, not that you were right. Avoid 'I never disagree' — it reads as either conflict-avoidant or unengaged.

---

### Q4. BFS vs DFS — when do you pick each?

**Answer.** BFS (queue) finds shortest unweighted paths and is level-order; DFS (stack/recursion) explores deep paths, suits topological sort, cycle detection in directed graphs, and tree traversal. DFS can also blow the stack on deep graphs — use an explicit stack if depth is unbounded.

---

### Q5. What is a skip list and why do databases use it?

**Answer.** A sorted linked list with extra 'express lanes' at higher levels, giving O(log n) expected search with much simpler, lock-friendly code than a balanced tree. LevelDB/RocksDB use it for their memtable because range scans are easy and concurrent insertion is cheap.

---

### Q6. What is a doubly linked list and what is its tradeoff?

**Answer.** Each node holds next and prev pointers, enabling O(1) removal if you have the node, and forward/backward iteration. Cost: 16 extra bytes per node and more pointer writes on mutation.

---

### Q7. How do balanced BSTs (AVL / red-black) stay balanced?

**Answer.** AVL enforces height difference <= 1 via rotations after inserts/deletes — tighter, better for search. Red-black allows larger imbalance but uses at most 2 rotations per insert, so it's favored when insert/delete dominate (e.g., Java TreeMap, Linux CFS).

---

### Q8. What is a sliding window and what does it solve?

**Answer.** Maintain [left, right) over an array with O(1) amortized adds/removes from the ends. Solves fixed-size window max/sum, and variable-size problems like 'longest substring without repeating chars' by shrinking when the constraint breaks.

---

### Q9. Explain quicksort and its worst case.

**Answer.** Pick a pivot, partition around it, recurse. Average O(n log n), in-place, cache-friendly. Worst case O(n^2) on sorted input with naive first/last pivot — fixed by randomized or median-of-three pivot. It's not stable.

---

### Q10. Why is merge sort O(n log n) always, and what does it cost?

**Answer.** It splits in half (log n levels) and each level merges in O(n). Guarantees n log n regardless of input and is stable, but needs O(n) auxiliary space — that's the cost.
