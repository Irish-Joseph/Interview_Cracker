# Daily Interview Prep — June 10, 2026

Focus: DS + algorithms. 2 questions worked through this session.

---

### Q1. What happens when you type a URL and press enter?

**Answer.** DNS resolution (cache -> resolver -> root/TLD/authoritative servers), TCP handshake with the server, then the TLS handshake (certificate validation, key exchange), then the HTTP request/response. The browser parses HTML, fetches sub-resources, and renders.

---

### Q2. What is a deadlock and what are its conditions?

**Answer.** Two or more threads each hold a resource the other needs, so none can proceed. Needs four conditions (Coffman): mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption, circular wait. Break any one — common practice: impose a global lock ordering to kill circular wait, or use try-lock with timeout/backoff.

---

### Q3. Explain how DNS works in a few sentences.

**Answer.** The resolver queries a recursive resolver, which walks the hierarchy: root servers point to the TLD (.com) servers, which point to the authoritative nameserver for the domain, which answers the A/AAAA/CNAME record. Results are cached at every level per TTL.

---

### Q4. What is the difference between a signal and an exception?

**Answer.** A signal is an asynchronous OS-level interrupt delivered to a process/thread (SIGTERM, SIGKILL, SIGSEGV); a process can install handlers or ignore it, but SIGKILL/SIGSTOP cannot be caught. An exception is a synchronous, in-language control flow mechanism for errors at a known instruction.

---

### Q5. What is the CAP theorem and what do real systems choose?

**Answer.** A distributed system cannot simultaneously guarantee Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance — and partitions happen, so the real choice is CP (refuse requests during a partition, e.g. ZooKeeper) or AP (serve stale data, e.g. DynamoDB).

---

### Q6. How does a load balancer work and what are the common algorithms?

**Answer.** It front-ends a pool of servers and routes each connection using round-robin, least-connections, or consistent hashing (minimizes cache invalidation when nodes change). L4 operates on IP/TCP; L7 inspects HTTP (path, headers) and can add TLS termination, retries, and rate limiting.

---

### Q7. Horizontal vs vertical scaling?

**Answer.** Vertical: bigger machine — simple but capped by hardware and a single point of failure. Horizontal: more machines — requires statelessness or sharded/replicated state, load balancing, and network-aware code, but scales past any single box and allows rolling deploys.

---

### Q8. What is sharding and how do you choose a shard key?

**Answer.** Partition data across databases by a key: hash sharding spreads evenly (but range queries hit all shards), range sharding keeps locality (but risks hot ranges). Choose the key from your query patterns — a hot key (e.g. one user's 1B feed) skews load, so you may double-key (user_id, post_id).

---

### Q9. Explain database replication and the consistency tradeoff.

**Answer.** A primary writes, followers apply via the write-ahead log (async: fast, can lose commits on failover; semi-sync: at least one follower acks; sync: safest, slowest). Reads can go to followers for scale but may return stale data — mitigate with read-your-writes, session tokens, or fencing.
