# Daily Interview Prep — August 17, 2026

Focus: databases. 1 question worked through this session.

---

### Q1. What does idempotency mean and how do you make writes idempotent?

**Answer.** Repeating the operation has no additional effect. Techniques: client-generated idempotency keys deduplicated server-side, PUT (replace, not append) semantics, and unique constraints (re-insert hits the constraint instead of duplicating). Needed for retries over networks where 'did it land?' is unknowable.

---

### Q2. What are dirty, non-repeatable and phantom reads?

**Answer.** Dirty read: reading another transaction's uncommitted data (lost if it rolls back). Non-repeatable read: re-reading a committed row that another committed transaction changed. Phantom read: a re-run of a range query returns new rows inserted by another committed transaction. Each maps to the isolation levels above.

---

### Q3. How does a JOIN actually execute?

**Answer.** The planner picks a strategy: nested loop (probe inner per outer row — good with cheap indexes or tiny outer), hash join (build a hash of the smaller side, probe with the larger — the default for large unindexed joins), or merge join (both sides sorted on the key — good for ranges). EXPLAIN shows which one and its estimated cost.

---

### Q4. What is an ORM and what are its classic performance problems?

**Answer.** A library mapping objects to tables (SQLAlchemy, Hibernate). Classic problems: the N+1 query (looping entities and loading each relation lazily — fix with eager join/IN batch), over-fetching with big eager graphs, writing the whole object back (lost updates on concurrent writes), and hiding the SQL you actually shipped.

---

### Q5. What is a foreign key and does it always need an index?

**Answer.** A constraint declaring that a column's values must exist in the parent table's key, with cascade options (RESTRICT/NO ACTION, CASCADE, SET NULL). Most DBs automatically index FK columns (PostgreSQL does; MySQL InnoDB does) because the check needs it — don't assume, check the catalog.

---

### Q6. Explain primary key choices: UUID vs auto-increment vs surrogate?

**Answer.** Auto-increment (BIGINT): tiny, fast, cluster-friendly on InnoDB, but predictable (scrapers) and hard to merge shards. UUIDv4: random, no central authority, but 16 bytes scatter B-tree pages (write amplification) — UUIDv7 (time-ordered) fixes locality. Surrogate keys decouple business identity from storage identity.

---

### Q7. What is transactional outbox and why use it?

**Answer.** To keep 'update DB + publish event' atomic: write the event into an outbox table in the same local transaction as the data change, then a relay (polling or CDC like Debezium) publishes it to the broker. Consumers deduplicate (at-least-once delivery) — this is the standard way to avoid 2PC across services.

---

### Q8. What is the STAR method and why do interviewers prefer it?

**Answer.** Situation, Task, Action, Result — a structured story where the interviewer can see context, your specific contribution (Action is where 'I', not 'we', belongs), and a measurable outcome. It forces concrete evidence over generic claims, which is what behavioral screens actually score.

---

### Q9. How do you answer 'Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate'?

**Answer.** Pick a real technical disagreement, show you steelmanned the other view, made the case with data/prototypes, and ended with a decision (yours or theirs) that you executed well. The signal is how you handle being wrong, not that you were right. Avoid 'I never disagree' — it reads as either conflict-avoidant or unengaged.

---

### Q10. How would you estimate a project nobody has built before?

**Answer.** Decompose into known sub-parts, size each (story points or days) with explicit unknowns called out, identify the riskiest item first (spike/prototype it), and present a range with confidence, not a point number. Then name the milestones that would let you re-estimate mid-project.

---

### Q11. Walk through your biggest production bug.

**Answer.** Structure: what it was, how you detected it (monitoring vs user report — be honest), your diagnosis path, the fix, and the systemic change you made so it can't recur (alert, test, runbook, migration guard). The last part is what senior interviewers are actually grading.

---

### Q12. Why do you want to work here / on this team?

**Answer.** Reference something specific you researched: their architecture posts, an open-source project, a product decision you respect, or a stated engineering value. Generic answers ('great company, good culture') signal you sent 50 applications. Match your stated goals to what the team actually needs.
